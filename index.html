Part 1

Embeddings from zero

The problem keyword search can’t solve, and the idea that solves it.

Say

No maths needed for this part. If you can picture points on a map, you can understand embeddings.

The problem

Keyword search misses most of these

Query

“cheap flights to Kochi”

Keyword search finds
  • Cheap flights to Kochi: deals
  • Budget airfare to COK
  • Low-cost tickets to Cochin
  • കൊച്ചിയിലേക്ക് കുറഞ്ഞ നിരക്കിൽ വിമാനം
Search by meaning finds
  • Cheap flights to Kochi: deals
  • Budget airfare to COK
  • Low-cost tickets to Cochin
  • കൊച്ചിയിലേക്ക് കുറഞ്ഞ നിരക്കിൽ വിമാനം

Synonyms (cheap / budget / low-cost), abbreviations (COK), spelling variants (Kochi / Cochin) and other languages all share a meaning, but not the same letters.

Say

Ask the room: “How many of these four would a classic search box find?” Only the first, because it is the only one that shares the exact words.

Understand it

Classic search (BM25, SQL LIKE, grep) counts overlapping words. It is fast and precise when people use the same words, and blind when they don’t. Embedding search compares meaning instead.

Q: Is keyword search obsolete then?No. Production systems often use both (“hybrid search”). Keywords are great for exact things like product codes and names; embeddings catch paraphrases.

Why start here

“768-dimensional vector” means nothing to most people. Building a 2-number embedding by hand makes every later idea obvious: dimensions are questions, similarity is distance, search is “nearest point”, and more questions means better separation.

Measuring closeness

Relevance is the angle between two arrows

Treat each embedding as an arrow from the origin. Arrows pointing the same way mean the same thing.

cos θ = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

The model normalizes vectors to length 1, so this is just the dot product: multiply matching numbers and add them up.

Say

Distance on the map and angle between arrows are two views of the same thing. We use the angle, called cosine similarity, because it ignores length and only cares about direction.

Understand it

  • The score runs from −1 to 1. In practice, real text embeddings mostly score between about 0.2 and 0.9; truly opposite vectors are rare.
  • Scores are relative: 0.55 is not “55% similar”. Use them to rank results, not as absolute truth, and pick thresholds by testing on your own data.
  • Because vectors are normalized, “cosine” and “dot product” give the same ranking, and dot products are very fast on any hardware.
Q: Cosine or Euclidean distance?For normalized vectors they give the same ranking. Use whatever your vector database defaults to, as long as it matches how the model was trained (cosine here).
How a model learns this

Training pulls matching pairs together

Positive pairs

A question and its answer, a caption and its photo, a sentence and its translation. Training moves them closer.

Negatives

Everything else in the batch. Training pushes them apart. This is called contrastive learning.

Solid dot = query, faded dot = its matching document. Repeat over millions of pairs and a map of meaning emerges.

Say

Nobody hand-writes these 768 numbers. We show the model millions of pairs that should match, and every time it puts a matching pair far apart, it gets corrected. Over time it learns a layout where meaning equals position.

Understand it

  • This is why multilingual works: training includes translation pairs, so “rain” and “മഴ” are pulled to the same place.
  • It’s also why multimodal works in EmbeddingGemma 2: image–caption and audio–text pairs pull different modalities into one shared space.
  • Google says EmbeddingGemma 2 comes from the same research as its Gemini Embedding models, and it’s built on the Gemma 4 architecture.
Q: Can I train it on my own data?Yes, fine-tuning with your own pairs (e.g. your FAQ questions → answers) is supported with sentence-transformers and Unsloth. Start without fine-tuning; only do it if evaluation on your data shows a gap.
Under the hood

How a sentence becomes a vector

1Prefix + text

task: search result | query: …

2Tokenizer

Splits text into sub-word pieces. 262K vocabulary, shared with Gemma 4

3Transformer

24 layers turn each token into a context-aware vector (width 512)

4Mean pooling

Average all token vectors into one

5Project + normalize

512 → 768 numbers, scaled to length 1

Images, audio, video

Have their own encoders (vision 170M, audio 300M parameters) that turn pixels and sound into “soft tokens”, which enter the same transformer at step 3.

One forward pass

Unlike a chatbot, there’s no word-by-word generation loop. One pass in, one vector out. That’s why it’s fast enough for phones.

Say

Five steps. The important ones for builders are 1 (you must add the right prefix) and 5 (output is always 768 numbers of length 1).

Understand it

  • Token: a chunk of text, roughly a word or part of a word. “Kubernetes” may be 3 tokens.
  • Context-aware: after the transformer, the vector for “bank” differs in “river bank” and “bank loan”.
  • Mean pooling: the simplest way to turn many token vectors into one: average them.
  • Sliding window 1024: most layers only look at nearby tokens (cheaper); every sixth layer looks at the whole input.
Q: Is it the same as Gemma 4 the chatbot?It shares the architecture family and tokenizer, but it’s trained for a different job: producing vectors rather than generating text.
Where embeddings fit

LLMs write answers. Embedding models find things.

Generative LLM (e.g. Gemma 4)
  • Produces text one token at a time
  • Good at reasoning and writing
  • Far too slow to read a million documents per question
Embedding model (EmbeddingGemma 2)
  • One forward pass gives one vector
  • Embed your documents once, store the vectors
  • Each query is one embedding plus fast maths
1 · Question

“What’s our leave policy?”

2 · Embedder

Finds the 5 most relevant passages

3 · LLM

Reads only those 5 and writes an answer

= RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation

Say

RAG is the most common reason people use embeddings today. The LLM is smart but can’t read your whole company wiki for every question. The embedder is the librarian that hands it the right five pages.

Understand it

Google pairs these on-device: EmbeddingGemma 2 for retrieval and Gemma 4 E2B for writing answers, both small enough for a phone. That’s a fully private RAG stack with no server at all.

Q: Why not just give the LLM a huge context window?Cost and speed: reading 1M tokens per question is slow and expensive, especially on a phone. Retrieval keeps the LLM’s input small and focused.

What it is, why running it on the device pays off, and the four tricks that make a 740M-parameter model fit in a phone.

Say

“Edge” just means the device in front of you: phone, laptop, browser, a small board. Not a data centre.

Released 6 October 2026 · Google DeepMind

EmbeddingGemma 2 at a glance

740Mparameters in total. The text-only core is just 270M.

Built on Gemma 4. Its predecessor, EmbeddingGemma (Sept 2025, 308M parameters, text only), passed 20 million downloads.

Say

Version 1 was text only. Version 2 adds images, audio and video, quadruples the context and switches to the fully open Apache 2.0 licence.

Understand it

  • Parameters: the learned numbers inside the model. More parameters means more memory. 740M is small by today’s standards: chat LLMs are often 7–70 billion.
  • Context window: the maximum input length. 8K tokens is roughly 6,000 English words, or a mix like 29 images.
  • Licence: v1 used the Gemma licence and needed you to accept terms on Hugging Face. v2 is Apache 2.0: download and ship it.
Q: Is there a bigger cloud version?Yes: Gemini Embedding 2, an API model with up to 3072 dimensions. Use it when you want maximum quality and don’t mind sending data to an API. EmbeddingGemma 2 is the one you can run yourself.
Why the edge

What you gain by embedding on the device

Privacy by default

Photos, voice notes, health and company data are embedded where they live. Nothing is uploaded, so there’s nothing to leak and no consent screen to design.

Milliseconds, not round-trips

About 8 ms per text on a Pixel 11 Pro. Fast enough to re-rank results on every keystroke, which is how the AI Edge Gallery app searches.

Zero cost per call

Index 10,000 notes or a whole photo library without an API bill. The user’s hardware does the work.

Works offline

On a flight, in a Munnar tea estate, in a basement server room. Search doesn’t depend on a signal.

Always fresh

New photos or messages are embedded the moment they arrive. No sync job, no stale server index.

Scales with users

A million users bring a million devices. Your server costs don’t grow with them.

Say

This is the core argument of the session. Pick the two that matter most for this audience: probably privacy and cost.

Understand it

  • Latency maths: a cloud API call typically adds a network round-trip on top of compute. On-device, the 8.3 ms (Pixel 11 Pro, text, from the LiteRT model card) is the whole cost.
  • Index maths: 10,000 notes × 8.3 ms ≈ 83 seconds to index everything on the phone.
  • Trade-off to admit honestly: the device has less memory and battery. That’s why the next four slides are about shrinking the model.
Q: What about battery?Embedding is a short burst (one forward pass), not a long generation loop. Index in the background while charging, like the photo apps already do for face grouping.

Say

Every parameter is a number, and every number costs bytes. Store each one in 4 bytes and the model is 3 GB, too big for a phone app. The rest of this section is how Google cuts that down.

Understand it

  • The 191 MB and 567 MB figures are from Google’s announcement. The LiteRT model card lists per-backend memory that varies (e.g. 85 MB text-only on iPhone 18 Pro GPU, 334 MB on Galaxy S26 Ultra CPU), because each backend handles memory differently.
  • Download sizes from the LiteRT model card: 165 MB (text), 388 MB (text + vision), 485 MB (everything).
Q: Will it fit on a cheap phone?The text-only build at about 200 MB fits on most modern phones. Test on the lowest-end device you want to support.
Edge trick 1

Quantization: fewer bits per number

bfloat16 · 16 bits

65,536 possible values per weight. What we use in Colab.

int8 · 8 bits

256 values. Used for the vision encoder. 2× smaller than bf16.

int4 · 4 bits

Just 16 values. Used for the text transformer. 4× smaller than bf16.

Plain quantization

Train normally, then round every weight to the nearest allowed value. Quick, but rounding errors can hurt quality.

Quantization-aware training (QAT)

Simulate the rounding during training, so the model learns to work with it. EmbeddingGemma 2 uses QAT for int4 and int8.

The LiteRT build mixes precisions: int4 for the transformer and embeddings, int8 for vision, a mix of int2/int4/int8 for audio, all per-channel (each output channel gets its own scale).

Say

Imagine storing prices. You could write ₹1,234.5678, or just round to ₹1,235. You lose a little precision but save a lot of space. Quantization rounds the model’s numbers.

Understand it

  • int4 means each weight can only be one of 16 values. That sounds brutal, but each group of weights gets its own scale factor (“per-channel”), so the 16 levels are spread where they’re needed.
  • QAT vs post-training: with QAT the model already “knows” it will be rounded, so it settles on weights that survive rounding. Quality loss is usually much smaller than rounding after the fact.
  • Google hasn’t published quality numbers for the quantized v2 bundle that I could find. Evaluate on your own data if accuracy matters.
Q: Can I quantize it myself?You don’t need to: Google ships pre-quantized .litertlm files, and the community has GGUF builds for llama.cpp and Ollama.

Rule of thumb

256-D is close to lossless for text. Keep 128-D for text-only work.

Say

Matryoshka are the Russian nesting dolls. The model is trained so the first 256 numbers are already a good embedding on their own, the first 512 a better one, and so on. You can just chop off the end.

Understand it

  • Storage maths: items × dimensions × 4 bytes (float32). 10,000 × 768 × 4 = 30.7 MB.
  • Shorter vectors also make each comparison faster, which matters when you scan thousands of items on a phone.
  • Multimodal quality drops faster at 128-D (45.65 vs 59.01), which is why the advice is 128 for text only.
  • Always re-normalize after truncating (sentence-transformers does it with normalize_embeddings=True).
Q: Can I mix 768-D and 256-D vectors?No. Pick one size for the whole index. You can truncate the stored 768-D vectors later without re-running the model, though.
Edge trick 4

Spend tokens wisely: one 8K budget for everything

1 / word piece

Text and code

280 / image

Default. ~29 images fit. Adjustable from 70 to 1120.

140 / frame

Video at 1 frame per second. ~58 frames fit.

25 / second

Audio, mono 16 kHz. ~5.5 minutes fit.

Fewer vision tokens = faster

Google’s latency tables use 70 tokens per image. Fine for “is this a beach photo?”, less detail for reading small text in a screenshot.

Adaptive graphs

The LiteRT runtime loads a graph sized to the actual input length and image size, so short inputs don’t pay for the full 8K.

Say

All inputs share one 8,192-token window. Images are the expensive part, so on a phone you turn the image detail down.

Understand it

  • Soft tokens: images and audio don’t have words, so the encoders turn them into vectors that the transformer treats like tokens.
  • The LiteRT bundle supports 70 or 140 image tokens; the full Python model lets you set 70 to 1120.
  • For long videos, embed segments (e.g. every 10 seconds) separately rather than one huge input. That’s how the Video Moments Finder returns timestamps.
Multimodal

One vector space for text, images, audio and video

Every input lands in the same 768-D space, so any modality can search any other. You can even mix them in a single input.

Say

This is what’s genuinely new in v2. Before, you needed separate models for text and images (like CLIP) and they lived in different spaces. Now one small model does all of it.

Understand it

  • <|image|> placeholders mark where each image sits in the text, so the model knows which caption belongs to which picture.
  • No prefix is used for images, audio and video: prefixes are for text only.

Benchmarks are standard test sets that everyone scores their models on. The headline is the code jump: almost 10 points. That makes it a strong choice for local code search and coding agents.

Understand it

  • Scores from different benchmarks aren’t comparable to each other. Compare models on the same benchmark only.

The #1 mistake

Tell the model what the vector is for

Use caseQuery sideDocument side
Searchtask: search result | query: …title: none | text: …
Question answeringtask: question answering | query: …title: none | text: …
Code retrievaltask: code retrieval | query: …title: {filename} | text: …
Classification, clustering, similaritytask: classification | query: … (same prefix on both sides)

In Python

Let sentence-transformers add them: prompt_name="SearchQuery" for queries, "Document" for documents.

Check your runtime’s docs

The LiteRT-LM guide writes them differently (e.g. task: search query | text: …). Use the format your runtime documents, and test.

Say

Queries and documents are different things: a query is short and a question, a document is long and an answer. Prefixes tell the model which one it’s looking at, so it can put them in the right place.

Understand it

  • Asymmetric tasks (search, Q&A) use different prefixes on each side. Symmetric tasks (clustering, similarity) use the same prefix for everything.
  • Forgetting prefixes doesn’t throw an error, it just quietly lowers quality. That’s why it’s the #1 mistake.
  • Honest note: the model card and the LiteRT-LM page show slightly different prefix wording. I’d follow the model card for Python and the LiteRT docs for LiteRT, and compare results on your own data.

How it works (say this)

Every intent gets a “prototype” vector: the average of 10 real example messages plus its name. A new message is embedded once and compared with all 77 prototypes. Highest score wins. If the top score is low or the runner-up is too close, it asks a clarifying question instead of guessing.

Real numbers (measured on this laptop, 7 Nov 2026)

  • Median 85 ms per message on the CPU; the server uses about 330 MB of RAM.
  • Names only already get 91.6%; adding 10 examples per intent gets 92.6%. Interesting point for the audience: a single example per intent is worse than the name alone (88.8%).
  • The confidence rule (top score ≥ 0.82 and ≥ 0.03 ahead of #2) keeps 87% of messages, and those are 97.5% right.

Honest caveats

  • Banking77 is a public benchmark the model may have seen in training. Expect lower on your own tickets.
  • Banking77 has no “off-topic” class; the 0.82 floor was tuned on a handful of hand-written off-topic messages like “what’s the weather in Kochi”.
  • The short Malayalam “പണം രണ്ടുതവണ പിടിച്ചു” comes out unsure (its top guess is still right); the longer phrasing on the example button is confident.
Q: Why not just use an LLM for this?An LLM would work, but it’s heavier and slower per message. Routing is a nearest-neighbour problem: one 85 ms embedding, then 77 dot products. Adding a new intent is adding a few example sentences, no retraining.
Demo 5 · Laptop, CPU only · apps/typed-assistant

Say it, get a typed call, or a question

The embedding model picks the function by meaning. Simple rules fill the arguments, and a validator checks every one against its type. It never runs an invalid call.

  1. “set the bedroom AC to 22 in cool mode” → set_ac(room="bedroom", temperature=22, mode="cool")
  2. “set AC to 40” → “Temperature must be between 16 and 30”
  3. “അമ്മയ്ക്ക് മെസ്സേജ് അയക്കൂ ഞാൻ വൈകും” → send_message(contact="Amma", …)

       temperature: int[16..30],
       mode: "cool" | "dry" | "fan" | "auto" = None)

           quantity: int[1..10])

Run it

apps/typed-assistant/run.sh → http://127.0.0.1:8091

About 90 ms per command on the CPU. No LLM involved.

Before the event

  • Run apps/typed-assistant/run.sh once at home: it embeds 95 texts (about 9 s) and caches them. Then it starts in about 0.3 s, fully offline.
  • Open http://127.0.0.1:8091. Enter runs a command; Alt+1…0 load the 10 examples; Alt+T toggles the typed tool list.

How it works (say this)

  1. Pick the function by meaning: each tool has a description plus 7–9 example phrases (English, Manglish, Malayalam) averaged into a prototype vector. The command is embedded once and compared with the 8 prototypes, the same idea as the intent matcher.
  2. Fill the arguments with plain rules: a synonym list for enums (with an embedding fallback: “onam feast” → sadya), regex for numbers, times and durations, keywords for on/off.
  3. Validate every argument against its type, range and allowed values. Anything wrong or missing becomes a clarifying question instead of a call. Follow-ups work: “set AC to 40” → “22” → “living room” runs the call.

Why “type-safe” matters

The output can only ever be one of 8 known functions with valid arguments, or a question. That’s the property you want before letting AI touch real devices or payments, and it runs in about 90 ms without sending anything off the laptop.

Real numbers (67 test utterances, measured on this laptop)

  • Main set (written alongside the tools): 100% tool choice and exact typed calls. That only shows the rules cover what they were written for.
  • Hard set (14 unseen phrasings): 64% confident and right. Every miss but one safely became “unsure” instead of a wrong call. That’s the honest number for an unprepared audience.
  • The one wrong call: “is the back door locked?” ran lock_door(back, locked=True), because there is no “check state” tool. Good talking point about schema design.

Known limits

  • Romanised Malayalam with no English keywords is weak (“Achanod parayu njan ethi” → unsure). Malayalam script works better.
  • One command per message; no dates (“tomorrow” stays in the reminder text). “turn off the AC” isn’t in the schema.
Q: Why not let an LLM write the JSON?You can, and with a small LLM you’d extract arguments better. But you still need the validator: models can produce out-of-range or made-up values. Here the embedder only chooses between known functions, so the worst case is a question, not a wrong action.
Putting it together

Anatomy of an on-device search app

When content arrives (once per item)
New item

Note, photo, PDF page, audio clip

Prepare

Split long text into chunks; resize images

Embed

Document prefix, 256-D

Store

id + vector in SQLite

When the user searches (every keystroke)
Query

Typed, spoken or a photo

Embed

Query prefix, same size

Compare

Dot product with stored vectors

Top-k

Show results, or pass to Gemma 4 E2B for an answer

For thousands of items, comparing against every vector is fine. For much larger collections, use an approximate nearest-neighbour index (MediaPipe Semantic Retriever, Qdrant, sqlite-vec).

Say

Every app we’ve shown follows this pattern. The expensive part (embedding documents) happens once; searching is cheap.

Understand it

  • Chunking: split long documents into pieces of a few hundred words, so each vector represents one idea. One vector for a 50-page PDF would be too blurry.
  • Brute force vs ANN: brute force compares the query with every stored vector: exact and simple. ANN (approximate nearest neighbour) indexes skip most comparisons: much faster at scale, very slightly less exact.
  • Keep the model fixed: if you upgrade the model or change dimensions, you must re-embed everything.

Before you ship

Six things to watch for

Use the task prefixes

Queries and documents need different ones. Missing prefixes fail silently.

bf16 or fp32, never fp16

float16 can produce NaN or degraded vectors without any error.

Same model on both sides

Changing model, build or dimensions means re-embedding the whole index.

8K tokens are shared

One image ≈ 280 tokens, one second of audio ≈ 25. Budget mixed inputs.

Quality varies by language

Evaluate on your own data, Malayalam included, before you promise anything.

Not safety-tuned

Add filtering and access control in your app, especially for RAG.

Understand it

  • The fp16 warning and “not safety-tuned” are both from the model card.
  • Access control in RAG: the embedder will happily retrieve a salary spreadsheet for an intern’s question. Filter search results by what the user is allowed to see before they reach the LLM.
